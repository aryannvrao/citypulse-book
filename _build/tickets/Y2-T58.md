### The ticket in plain words

Many employers run structured interviews. The interviewer asks about things you have actually done, such as "tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate" or "describe a failure you fixed". They want one specific past example, what you personally did in it, and what happened as a result.

CityPulse needs this ticket because the squad's work only helps you in an interview if you can explain your own part of it. Over six months you have had real incidents: a deploy you rolled back, a duplicate-rows bug caught by a data-quality check, a CI pipeline that broke, and rework after a mentor review. Each one is a story. This ticket asks you to choose five of them, write each in a fixed structure called STAR, link each one to proof, and record one answer of no more than two minutes.

The key idea is the word "I". A team story is easy to tell and hard to check. A story that names your actions, with evidence that shows them, proves ownership.

### Words you need

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| STAR | A story structure: Situation, Task, Action, Result. |
| Behavioural interview | An interview where you answer "tell me about a time when..." with a real past example. |
| Action | What you personally did. This is where the "I" verbs go. |
| Evidence link | A web address that proves the story, such as a pull request, an issue or a CI run. |
| Pull request (PR) | A proposed set of changes on GitHub, which others review before it is merged. |
| Commit | A saved set of changes in Git, with a message. |

| Rollback | Returning a system to an earlier, working version after a bad release. |
| DQ check | A data-quality check: a rule that tests data, such as "no duplicate rows". |
| Rework | Changing finished work because a reviewer or mentor found a problem. |
| Partner | The student who listens to your recorded answer and scores it with the rubric. |
| Rubric | The scoring guide the partner uses, from 0 to 5. |
| mp4 | A common video file format. |
| ffprobe | A tool that reads information about a media file, such as its length, without playing it. |

| Rollno | Your roll number, used in your file name. |

### How your trainer checks it

Your trainer checks five acceptance criteria and four test cases. Check each one yourself before the trainer does.

**Acceptance criterion 1: five stories, one per category, each with a numeric result and an evidence link.** Count the stories and check each category is different. Your trainer sets the five categories; if you cannot find the list on the board, ask before you write anything. Each Result must contain a number, such as a count, a percentage, minutes or failed runs.

**Acceptance criterion 2: each Action section lists at least three first-person actions.** "First-person" means the sentence has "I" as its subject, such as "I wrote the rollback script". Sentences beginning "We" or "The team" do not count.

**Acceptance criterion 3: the recording is no more than 2:00 and answers a bank question in STAR order.** Play it. It must stop before 2:00 and go Situation, Task, Action, Result.

**Acceptance criterion 4: the partner rubric score is at least 3 out of 5, with the partner's name.** Both appear together on the submitted form.

**Acceptance criterion 5: three interviewer questions are listed in the doc footer.**

The trainer then runs four test cases, in order.

**TC1: open the LMS STAR doc, count stories and check the R column.** Expected: five stories, and each Result has a number. It fails if you count four or six stories, or if any Result says only "it was better" with no figure.

**TC2: click two evidence links at random.** Expected: each opens a real pull request, issue or CI run involving you. It fails if a link gives a 404 page, needs access the trainer lacks, or opens a teammate's work.

**TC3: run ffprobe on the recording.** The trainer runs:

```bash
ffprobe -show_entries format=duration Y2-T58_<rollno>.mp4
```

Break it into parts:

- `ffprobe` is the program.
- `-show_entries format=duration` asks only for the `duration` field in the file's `format` section.
- `Y2-T58_<rollno>.mp4` is your recording, with your roll number in place of `<rollno>`.

This prints a `[FORMAT]` block with a `duration=` line, in seconds. For a quicker check that prints only the number, add `-v error` (hide the banner) and `-of default=noprint_wrappers=1:nokey=1`. Passing output looks like this:

```term
$ ffprobe -v error -show_entries format=duration -of default=noprint_wrappers=1:nokey=1 Y2-T58_<rollno>.mp4
118.736000
```

A duration of 118.736 seconds passes, because it is 120 seconds or less. A failing output such as `131.200000` usually means you talked past the question. Trim the file or record again, as described in stage 5.

**TC4: the trainer asks "What exactly did YOU do?" about one story.** Expected: you name specific personal actions, such as a commit you wrote, a review you did or a decision you made, not what the team did. It fails if your answer is "we did..." or "the team did..." and you cannot say which step was yours.

### Do it, step by step

#### Stage 1: find candidate moments (Y2-T58.1)

Goal: six to eight moments where something went wrong, was fixed or was improved, and you were involved.

First, list your recent commits. Run this from inside the squad repository, in Git Bash or on the Linux lab machine:

```bash
git log --author="$(git config user.name)" --since="6 months ago" --date=short --pretty=format:"%h %ad %s"
```

Line by line:

- `git log` lists the commit history.
- `--author="$(git config user.name)"` keeps only commits made under your Git name. `$( )` inserts the output of `git config user.name`.
- `--since="6 months ago"` limits the list to recent work.
- `--date=short` prints dates such as `2026-03-14`.
- `--pretty=format:"%h %ad %s"` prints one line per commit: short hash, date and subject.

Expected output: one line per commit, such as `a1b2c3d 2026-03-14 Fix duplicate rows in loader`. If it is empty, check that `git config user.name` prints your name and that you are in the squad repository.

Second, list your pull requests with `gh`:

```bash
gh pr list --author @me --state all --limit 30
```

`--author @me` means the account you are logged in with, and `--state all` includes open, closed and merged pull requests. Expected output: a table of numbers, titles and branches. Note the ones that look like incidents.

Third, check the CI history:

```bash
gh run list --limit 20
```

A red status is a candidate: CI broke and someone fixed it. To read the failed log of one run, use `gh run view <run-id> --log-failed`, replacing `<run-id>` with the number from the list.

Fourth, read your incident notes and write down every rollback, DQ check, CI failure or mentor rework you find.

Test this stage: you should have six to eight candidates, each saying what happened, when, and which link proves it.

Here is a simple way to count first-person actions in one story. The trainer reads by eye; this code only shows the idea. Read it, then press play:

```trace
TITLE: Counting first-person actions in one story
CODE:
actions = ["I wrote the retry check", "We reviewed the PR", "I rolled back the deploy"]
count = 0
for line in actions:
    if line.startswith("I "):
        count = count + 1
STEPS:
1 :: actions=["I wrote the retry check", "We reviewed the PR", "I rolled back the deploy"]; count=0 :: The first line builds the list of three action sentences.
2 :: actions=["I wrote the retry check", "We reviewed the PR", "I rolled back the deploy"]; count=0 :: The counter starts at zero.
3 :: line="I wrote the retry check"; count=0 :: The loop takes the first sentence.
4 :: line="I wrote the retry check"; count=0 :: startswith("I ") is True for this sentence.
5 :: line="I wrote the retry check"; count=1 :: The counter goes up by one.
3 :: line="We reviewed the PR"; count=1 :: The loop takes the second sentence.
4 :: line="We reviewed the PR"; count=1 :: startswith("I ") is False, so line 5 is skipped.
3 :: line="I rolled back the deploy"; count=1 :: The loop takes the third sentence.
4 :: line="I rolled back the deploy"; count=1 :: startswith("I ") is True again.
5 :: line="I rolled back the deploy"; count=2 :: The counter reaches 2 out of 3 first-person sentences.
```

#### Stage 2: pick five moments and fill the STAR table (Y2-T58.2)

Goal: five stories, one per category, each in a STAR table.

Choose from your candidates. Each story must cover one category from the trainer's list, with no category used twice; involve at least one clear action of yours; and have evidence you can link.

Give each story a table with four rows: Situation (one or two sentences: where and what problem), Task (one sentence: your responsibility), Action (three to five lines, one personal step each) and Result (one or two sentences with a number and a link).

Here is a fictional example about a different project, to show the shape. Do not copy it. A student on a bike-share price app found that the nightly job sent two prices for each bike before a Friday release.

- Situation: The nightly price job produced double rows on release day.
- Task: Find and fix the cause, because the release depended on it.
- Action: I reran the job on a copy of the data. I found the join that matched each bike twice. I added a unique-key check to the pipeline.
- Result: Duplicate prices fell from 214 rows to 0 over the next three nightly runs. Evidence: link to the merged pull request.

Write your own version in your own words.

Test this stage: read each Situation aloud. Each table has four rows.

#### Stage 3: add numbers, evidence and first-person actions (Y2-T58.3)

Goal: every Result has a number and a link, and every Action has at least three "I" sentences.

For numbers, go back to the source: a test count, row count, run count or time in minutes. If you only have "it got better", look in the CI run or the data-check output. Write the number with its unit, such as "47 failed runs before, 0 after".

For links, open each pull request or CI run and copy its address. A pull request looks like `https://github.com/<owner>/citypulse-<squad>/pull/42`, and a CI run like `https://github.com/<owner>/citypulse-<squad>/actions/runs/<number>`. Replace the placeholders with real values, and click each link once.

For actions, rewrite every sentence that is not about you so it starts with "I" and names a verb such as wrote, reviewed, reran, measured or rolled back. If the evidence shows someone else did a step, keep that as a team fact and describe what you did.

Test this stage: run the counting idea from stage 1 on each Action, then open every link and confirm it involves you.

#### Stage 4: pair practice (Y2-T58.4)

Goal: answer three bank questions out loud, and get scored with the rubric.

A bank question is a question an interviewer asks, such as "tell me about a time you made a mistake". Use the question bank from your trainer. If you cannot find it, ask before you start.

For each answer, your partner starts a timer when you begin, stops it when you finish, writes down the time, and scores you with the rubric. Swap roles after three answers.

Test this stage: each answer finishes within two minutes and goes in STAR order. If your partner says you started with the result, practise again with the Situation first.

#### Stage 5: record the best answer (Y2-T58.5)

Goal: a recording of 2:00 or less, saved as `Y2-T58_<rollno>.mp4`, with your partner's score.

Record your best answer in one take, without notes. Check the length with the same command the trainer uses, in the short form:

```bash
ffprobe -v error -show_entries format=duration -of default=noprint_wrappers=1:nokey=1 raw-answer.mp4
```

The output is the length in seconds. If it is above 120, trim a copy, not the original:

```bash
ffmpeg -i raw-answer.mp4 -t 115 -c:v libx264 -c:a aac Y2-T58_<rollno>.mp4
```

Line by line:

- `ffmpeg` is the program.
- `-i raw-answer.mp4` is the input file.
- `-t 115` keeps the first 115 seconds, a safe margin under 120.
- `-c:v libx264` re-encodes the video with the common H.264 codec.
- `-c:a aac` re-encodes the sound with AAC, which mp4 files commonly use.
- `Y2-T58_<rollno>.mp4` is the output file.

Expected result: a new file in the same folder. Run the ffprobe check again. Trimming can cut the end of your Result, so listen once to be sure the answer still ends properly. If it does not, record again and speak more briskly.

Upload the file as the ticket says, with your partner's score of at least 3 out of 5 and the partner's name.

#### Stage 6: three interviewer questions (Y2-T58.6)

Goal: three questions you would ask an interviewer, at the end of the doc.

Good questions are specific and open. Cover three areas: how the team works day to day, how success is measured in the first months, and what the team wants to improve next.

Test this stage: each question ends with a question mark, and no two are the same.

### What goes wrong

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| ffprobe prints more than 120. | The recording is too long, or you talked past the question. | Trim a copy with `ffmpeg -t 115`, or record again with the extra detail removed. |
| `git log --author` prints nothing. | Your Git name does not match the commits, or you are in the wrong folder. | Compare `git config user.name` with `git log --format=%an -n 5`, and change into the squad repository. |
| An evidence link opens a teammate's work. | You copied a link from a teammate's story, or the change was a team change. | Replace it with a link where you are the author, or where your review or commit is clearly visible. |
| An Action has fewer than three "I" sentences. | You wrote it as a team report, with "we" and "the team". | Rewrite each sentence to start with "I" and name a verb you did. |
| A Result has no number. | You described it in words only, such as "it was better". | Find the figure in the CI run, data check or pull request, and write it with its unit. |
| Partner score is 2 out of 5 or lower. | The answer was not in STAR order, or the Action was unclear. | Practise with the Situation first and speak the Action clearly. Record a new version. |
| Trainer's "What did YOU do?" gets "the team" as the answer. | You learned the story from the squad, not from your own actions. | Go back to your commits and reviews and name the exact thing you did, or choose another story. |
| Two stories share a category. | You did not check the category list before choosing. | Swap one for a candidate that covers the missing category. |

### Defend it in the viva

Question 1: Why does each story need a number and an evidence link, rather than a clear description alone?

:::answer Model answer
A description can be true or false, and the interviewer cannot check it. A number makes the result specific, and a link lets someone open the pull request or CI run and see it. Together they show the story is real and that you measure your own work. The trainer clicks links at random for the same reason: to test the claim against the evidence.
:::

Question 2: What is the trade-off between a short Situation and a long one?

:::answer Model answer
A short Situation reaches the Action quickly, which suits a two-minute answer, but the listener may not see why the problem mattered. A longer Situation gives context and uses time that should go to your Action and Result. Keep the Situation to one or two sentences, and give the Action most of the time. Judge the balance by timing your practice runs.
:::

Question 3: What did you personally do in your strongest story?

:::answer Model answer
Answer with specific steps in the first person, and tie each step to evidence. Name what you ran, found, wrote and reviewed. Do not describe team steps as your own, and do not add steps you did not take. The trainer will ask follow-up questions, and only real detail holds up.
:::

Question 4: Why do the partner and the rubric matter?

:::answer Model answer
The partner is a second listener who measures the answer against a fixed scale, so the score is less of a personal opinion. Practice with a partner also shows where an answer is unclear, such as a missing Result or a long Situation. A score of at least 3 out of 5 shows the answer is clear, and the feedback shows what to improve.
:::

Question 5: What would you do if asked about a failure you caused?

:::answer Model answer
Choose a story where you caused the problem. Say what you got wrong without blaming the team, then explain how you found and fixed it, and what check or review you added so it does not happen again. Show the evidence for each step. Interviewers often value an honest failure story, because it shows how you respond when things go wrong.
:::
