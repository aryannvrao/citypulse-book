### The ticket in plain words

Demo Day 2 is the second checkpoint of the course. Your squad stands in front of a panel of Atria faculty and industry or hiring partners and shows what it built since Demo Day 1. The panel scores how much the squad has grown, and it scores each intern on speaking and on answers to individual questions. Your squad's main project is CityPulse, a platform that collects and shows Bengaluru city data. Your squad also shows the industry mini-project, the piece signed off at Y2-D07.

The score that matters most for you is CRI 2. CRI is the course's checkpoint score, worked out from weighted components. The target is at least 68. You do not see the total on the day. Your private slip shows the total and the breakdown, so you can see which part pulled you down. If your score is below 68, you sign a one-week improvement plan.

The ticket also asks for an action log. This is a file called `docs/action-log.md` in your squad repository. It records the panel's feedback word for word, and it shows which items have been closed. Each closed item must link to evidence the trainer can open, such as a commit or a pull request dated today.

Why does CityPulse need this? Hiring partners use the day to build a fair shortlist for placements, so they need to see what each student did, not only what the group did. Feedback is worth nothing if nobody acts on it or can show that they did. The description names no specific failure, so think about the risk it guards against: a group that presents well but cannot say who built which part, and feedback that is forgotten by the next morning.

### How your trainer checks it

The trainer checks five acceptance criteria. Each criterion is a sentence that starts with "Given" (the starting state), "when" (the moment you are checked) and "then" (what must be true). Read them as a checklist.

First, when the panel slot ends, every squad member must have a speaking score and an individual Q&A score on the panel sheet. Second, at 13:00 every student must have a CRI 2 value with its component breakdown delivered privately. Third, at 15:30 `action-log.md` must hold verbatim panel feedback, with at least one item per student marked done and an openable evidence link. Fourth, any student below 68 must have a signed one-week plan on the LMS, with specific checkable actions and a T61 review date. Fifth, when the day ends, the shortlist must be shared with the programme office in the agreed format.

Then the four test cases. Each one says what the trainer does and what they expect.

**TC1: the panel scoring sheet.** The trainer opens the panel scoring sheet, filtered to your squad. There is no terminal command here, so the steps are the filter. Filtering means showing only the rows where the squad column equals your squad name. Expected: five students, each with a speaking score and a Q&A score, and the growth-versus-DD1 column filled. Passing looks like five rows with no blank scores. Failing looks like a blank growth cell, which usually means the DD1 score was never copied across, or fewer than five rows, which means a member is missing from the sheet and the Tech Lead must tell the trainer before the slot ends.

**TC2: the action log.** The trainer opens `citypulse-<squad>/docs/action-log.md` and clicks each item marked done. You can rehearse this before the trainer does. Count the done rows in a terminal. The stage that builds the table is in the step-by-step section, so here you only need to see the check working.

```term
$ cd citypulse-<squad>
$ grep -c "| done |" docs/action-log.md
5
```

`cd` moves into the repository folder. `grep -c` counts the lines that match a pattern and prints the count. The pattern `| done |` matches a table cell that holds exactly the word done, surrounded by pipes and spaces. The filename comes last, so grep knows where to look. Passing output is a number of at least 5, one per student. Failing output of 3, for example, means that two students have no done row. The usual cause is a status cell spelled "Done", "done." or "closed", which the pattern does not match, so use the exact lowercase word. Each done link should open a commit, pull request or résumé upload dated today. A 404 page means the link is wrong or private. Open it in your logged-in browser, not with `curl`, because `curl` is anonymous and gets a 404 for private pages it cannot see.

**TC3: the LMS plan.** The trainer filters the LMS for CRI 2 below 68 and checks for an item called "Y2-T60 1-week plan". There is no command. Passing means each affected student has a signed plan, with actions that name what will be done and when, and a T61 review date. Failing looks like an unsigned plan, actions such as "study more" with no date or measure, or no review date. Fix the wording so that a stranger could check each action.

**TC4: the programme-office share log.** The trainer checks the share log. Expected: the shortlist sent on 24 April, with its criteria and evidence links. Passing means the criteria columns and the evidence links are present, and the file uses the programme office's agreed format. Failing looks like a shortlist sent without criteria, sent to the wrong address, or sent in a different format. Check the format with the programme office contact before you send, and never send a student's private CRI breakdown in it.

### Do it, step by step

#### Y2-T60.1: check the deck and videos on the backup laptop

**Goal.** By 09:00 the deck PDF and every backup video are on the backup laptop, and the running order names every member's part.

**Stage 1: find the files.** On the backup laptop, in Git Bash, list the demo folder:

```
ls -lh ~/demo
```

`ls` lists files. `-l` shows the size and date, and `-h` makes sizes readable, such as `84M`. `~/demo` is your demo folder, which you should replace with the folder your squad agreed. Expected output: the deck PDF and each video file, with sizes that are not zero. A zero-size file is a failed copy, so copy it again.

**Stage 2: check the copy matches the original.** Compare fingerprints. A SHA-256 hash is a short code that changes if even one byte changes. Run the same command on the original and on the backup laptop, then compare the two codes. In Git Bash:

```
sha256sum ~/demo/deck.pdf
```

`sha256sum` prints the hash, then the filename. Expected output: a long code of letters and digits, followed by the path. Matching codes mean the copies are identical. On Windows PowerShell the equivalent is `Get-FileHash -Algorithm SHA256 path\to\deck.pdf`, which prints a `Hash` value you compare the same way. Replace `deck.pdf` with your deck's real filename.

**Stage 3: write the running order.** Make a plain table with four columns: order, speaker, part, minutes. The minutes must add up to 15. The ticket's sections are problem, CityPulse journey, mini-project, impact, limits and growth since DD1, so each row names one of those. Keep the table in a document the squad can see.

**Test this stage.** Open each file on the backup laptop without internet. If the video plays and the deck shows every slide, the stage is done.

#### Y2-T60.2: build the 15-minute talk

**Goal.** A running order that the squad can present in 15 minutes.

**Stage 1: time each part.** Read your part out loud with a phone timer and write the time beside each row. Speaking aloud gives a truer time than thinking through the words.

**Stage 2: cut to fit.** If the total is over 15 minutes, shorten the slides, not the points. Each part should answer one question: what was the problem, what did we build, what did it change, what does it still not do.

**Test this stage.** Present once in full to a squad-mate, who times you. Expected: between 14 and 15 minutes.

#### Y2-T60.3: prepare your part and your evidence

**Goal.** You present one part and can answer one individual question about your own work, with evidence.

**Stage 1: pick your part.** Choose the part you built, not one you only watched. Your commit history must show that work. You can check which commits are yours with this command, run in your repository:

```
git log --author="Your Name" --oneline
```

`git log` lists commits. `--author` keeps only commits whose author name contains the text you give. `--oneline` prints each commit as a short ID and its message on one line. Expected output: your commits, most recent first. If the list is empty, the name does not match your Git name; check it with `git config user.name`.

**Stage 2: prepare your evidence.** For each claim in your part, find the file or commit that proves it. Put the link in your notes. An evidence link looks like `https://github.com/<owner>/citypulse-<squad>/commit/<full-sha>`. Get the full commit ID with `git rev-parse HEAD`, which prints the ID of the commit you are on.

**Stage 3: prepare for one question.** Write down the question you expect, such as "why did you choose this approach?", and answer it in two sentences out loud. Answer from your own repository, not from memory.

**Test this stage.** Ask a squad-mate to pick a line from your part and ask you about it. If you can point to the file and the commit within 30 seconds, you are ready.

#### Y2-T60.4: write action-log.md

**Goal.** A file called `docs/action-log.md` in the squad repository, with every panel feedback item written verbatim, its type, its owner and its due date.

**Why this file matters.** The trainer checks it at 15:30 and clicks its evidence links. A log that paraphrases the panel fails, because the ticket asks for verbatim feedback.

**Stage 1: create the branch.** In your repository, start from an up-to-date main, then make your branch. Run these in Git Bash:

```
git switch main
git pull --ff-only
git switch -c feat/y2-t60-<your-handle>
```

`git switch main` moves to the main line. `git pull --ff-only` downloads the latest changes and refuses to continue if they cannot be applied as a simple fast-forward, which protects you from accidental merge commits. `git switch -c` creates a new branch and moves onto it in one step. Expected output: a line saying you switched to a new branch. If `pull` complains about divergence, stop and ask the Tech Lead, because nothing should be on main that you did not expect.

**Stage 2: create the file with the table header.** Create `docs/action-log.md` with a short title and this structure. The columns are the structure; the rows are yours to fill.

| # | Feedback (verbatim) | Type | Owner | Due | Status | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | | | | | | |

The `#` column numbers the items. The feedback cell holds the panel's exact words. Type is quick, medium or structural. Owner is one student's name. Due is a date. Status is open, in progress or done, written in lowercase. Evidence is the link you will add when the item is closed.

Here is a fictional example from a different project, a bike-share app, so you can see the shape. Do not copy it into your log.

| # | Feedback (verbatim) | Type | Owner | Due | Status | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "The station map is hard to read on a phone." | quick | Asha | 18:00 | open | |

**Stage 3: fill in the rows.** Copy each panel comment exactly from your notes. Do not tidy the grammar. Then classify each one: a quick fix takes minutes, a medium fix takes about a day, a structural fix needs a redesign. Each item needs one owner. Give each item a realistic due date; ask your trainer if a structural item needs a date after T61.

**Stage 4: commit and push.** Save, then run:

```
git add docs/action-log.md
git commit -m "docs(y2-t60): add action-log.md with verbatim panel feedback"
git push -u origin feat/y2-t60-<your-handle>
```

`git add` stages the file for the next commit. `git commit -m` saves it with the message in quotes. `git push -u` uploads your branch to `origin` and remembers the link, so later pushes need no extra words. Expected output: a line from GitHub with a link to create a pull request.

**Test this stage.** Run `grep -c "^| [0-9]" docs/action-log.md`. The output must equal the number of panel feedback items you wrote down. The pattern matches table rows that start with a number.

#### Y2-T60.5: close a feedback item with evidence

**Goal.** At least one panel feedback item closed today in your repository or résumé, with an evidence link, and marked done in the log.

This is the subtask where your own commits count. Choose an item you can close in about an hour, and make the change in your own area of the squad repository.

**Stage 1: make the change on your own branch.** Make a small, focused change. Keep the change to the one item. Do not bundle other fixes in, because the reviewer must see one clear diff.

**Stage 2: check the change is yours and dated today.** Use this command, replacing the name with yours:

```
git log --since="$(date +%F) 00:00" --author="Your Name" --oneline
```

`date +%F` prints today's date in the form `2026-11-17` (your own output will show the real date of the day you run it). The `$( ... )` part runs that command and places its output in the string. `--since` keeps commits made from midnight today. Expected output: at least one commit line. An empty list means your commit is not dated today, so make a new commit rather than changing an old one.

**Stage 3: open a pull request and get an approval.** Push your branch, then open a pull request on GitHub into main. Link the panel item in the PR description. Ask a squad-mate to review. You may not merge your own pull request, and the review must be approved before merging. The trainer checks the approval in the PR's history.

**Stage 4: write the evidence link and mark the row done.** Get the full commit ID with `git rev-parse HEAD`. Build the link as `https://github.com/<owner>/<repo>/commit/<full-sha>` or use the PR's own address, which looks like `.../pull/<number>`. Edit your row so its status cell reads `done` and its evidence cell holds the link. Commit and push the log change with a message such as `docs(y2-t60): mark item 2 done with evidence link to the commit`.

:::trap Use the full commit ID
Short IDs can work in Git, but a GitHub link should use the full ID from `git rev-parse HEAD`. A short one may point at the wrong commit once more history arrives. Push the commit before you link it, and take the ID before you make the log-edit commit. If your squad squash-merges, the PR address is the safer evidence link.
:::

```flow
TITLE: How a closed item reaches the trainer

STEPS:
1>2 :: verbatim feedback :: The panel's words are copied exactly into the log.
2>3 :: item to close :: You make one small change and commit it today.
3>2 :: evidence link :: You add the link and set the status to done.

```

**Test this stage.** Run the TC2 count from earlier. It must go up by one. Then click your own link in a browser that is logged in to GitHub. It should open your commit or PR.

#### Y2-T60.6: read your private CRI 2 slip

**Goal.** A short note of the gap between your CRI 2 and 68, by component.

The slip is private. Do not post it in the squad channel and never commit it to the repository. Put your note in your own notes.

**Stage 1: read the total and components.** Note the total and the weight of each component. The slip is the authority; if a number is unclear, ask the trainer.

**Stage 2: work out the gap for each component.** For each component, the gap is the points you lost, multiplied by that component's weight. Here is a fictional slip, with three made-up components, so you can see the arithmetic. It is not the real slip.

```python
weights = {"work": 0.5, "viva": 0.3, "tests": 0.2}
lost = {"work": 10, "viva": 25, "tests": 5}
gaps = {name: weights[name] * lost[name] for name in weights}
print(sorted(gaps.items(), key=lambda kv: kv[1], reverse=True))
```

Running it in Python 3 prints the components sorted by weighted gap, largest first: `[('viva', 7.5), ('work', 5.0), ('tests', 1.0)]`. Put your own weights and points in place of these. The two largest weighted gaps are the ones your plan will target in T60.7.

**Test this stage.** Usually your two largest gaps add up to a big share of your total gap. If they do not (for example, five components all lost similar points), that is fine: the plan still targets the top two, so just recheck that your arithmetic matches the slip.

#### Y2-T60.7: sign a one-week plan if you are below 68

### Defend it in the viva

The trainer will ask questions about this ticket. Practise these out loud, in your own words.

**Question 1 (why): Why does the action log need an evidence link for each done item, when a verbal "done" would be quicker?**

:::answer Model answer
A verbal claim cannot be checked later, and the panel and the programme office rely on the record after the day ends. A link to a commit or pull request dated today lets anyone open the work and see what changed and when. It also ties the claim to a person, because commits show an author. The link is the evidence that the item was closed, not only the word "done".
:::

**Question 2 (trade-off): For a feedback item, when would you choose a quick fix and when a structural change, given you have one working day?**

:::answer Model answer
A quick fix closes the item today and shows progress, which is what the panel scores when it looks at growth. A structural change may address the root cause better, but it may not finish in a day, and an unfinished change is worse evidence than a small closed one. A good answer weighs the item's type from the log, the time left before 15:30, and whether the fix can be reviewed in that time. Choose the smallest change that truly answers the feedback, and say what is left for later.
:::

**Question 3 (what you did): Which feedback item did you close, and how do a reviewer and the trainer confirm it?**

:::answer Model answer
Give the item's number and its wording from the log, then name the commit or pull request that closed it. Say what the change was in one sentence, who reviewed and approved it, and where the evidence link is in the log. This answer must describe your own work from your repository. The structure is the point: item, change, review, link.
:::

**Question 4 (understanding): Why is your CRI 2 breakdown kept private, and what does the squad see instead?**

:::answer Model answer

**Question 5 (why a plan targets two gaps): Why does the one-week plan target the two largest weighted gaps rather than every component?**

:::answer Model answer
A week is short, so a plan with too many actions cannot be done well or checked properly. The largest weighted gaps move the total most, so two well-chosen actions give the biggest gain for the time. Each action must still be checkable, so the trainer can see whether it happened at the T61 review.
:::
