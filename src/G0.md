## What you are being scored on

The course site was read to find out what it measures. Every one of the 29 days has the same five items, and six days also carry a milestone project.

| Item | What it is | How it is marked |
|---|---|---|
| Slides and "Technology in Action" workbook | The lesson and a worked example | Completion only |
| Hands-on lab | A guided exercise in 7 steps | You follow it and check your own result |
| Day quiz | 10 case-study multiple-choice questions on 10 named topics | Pass mark 70% |
| Graded lab | A Python problem: fill in a few functions | Visible tests plus hidden tests, out of 100 |
| Milestone project brief (days 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 29) | A guided build, then five "Part G" challenges | A reading item; the same skills are marked through the capstone tickets |
| Capstone ticket (63 of them, Y2-T01 to Y2-T63) | Real squad work on the capstone board, about one per session | Your trainer runs the ticket's test cases and gives a gate score from 0 to 3 |

On top of those, there is a viva scored out of 20, and all ticket work is judged from the evidence in your Git history. The section "The capstone board" below explains how.

:::key The rule that decides rankings
Your skill score uses your **first attempt**. You may retry a quiz up to ten times, but the first try is the one that is recorded for skills. So the winning habit is simple: never open a quiz or a graded lab until you have finished that day's chapter and scored at least 9 out of 10 on its practice quiz here.
:::

## Three facts that change how you study

**Every graded lab is Python.** Even on the Git day, the Linux day and the Docker day, the graded lab asks you to write Python functions about that day's idea: parse the output of a command, evaluate a policy, lint a file. If you cannot yet write a loop and a dictionary, read Part 1 before anything else. It is the highest-value part of the book.

**Hidden tests carry up to half the marks.** The visible tests on a graded lab are worth between 50 and 85 marks out of 100. The rest come from tests you cannot see, and those check the awkward inputs: an empty list, a tie, a missing field. Chapter F4 teaches a routine for finding those cases before the platform does.

**Quizzes go beyond the slides.** Each quiz has ten named topics, and several are not on the slides at all. Day 7's quiz, for example, covers generators, dataclasses and caching, which the Day 7 slides barely touch. Every day chapter in this book has a section called "What the quiz tests" with one part for each of the ten topics.

## The daily routine

```flow
TITLE: One course day, in the order that protects your first attempt
NODES: Read the chapter | Do the lab | Practice quiz here | Practice problem | Real quiz | Real graded lab
STEPS:
1>2 :: understand :: Read the day's chapter, then do the hands-on lab on the platform with the chapter's lab walkthrough open beside it.
2>3 :: check :: Take the chapter's ten-question practice quiz. Below 9 out of 10, re-read the topics you missed and try again.
3>4 :: code :: Solve the chapter's practice problem yourself before opening the answer. It trains the same skills as the graded lab.
4>5 :: first attempt :: Only now open the real day quiz. Read each scenario twice. This attempt is the one that counts.
5>6 :: first attempt :: Open the graded lab. List the edge cases, write your own tests, then submit once.
6>1 :: commit :: Commit your work under your own name, and start the next day.
```

## The capstone board

This was checked on the course site (learner view, 17 November 2026). It is the part of the course that most resembles the internship itself, and it is where most of your final grade comes from.

Your squad (about five students) shares one board of 63 tickets, scheduled roughly one per session from Y2-T01 (onboarding with 2FA) to Y2-T63 (a hiring-day simulation). Every ticket has:

- a **user story** ("As the CityPulse operations lead, I want ... so that ...");
- **acceptance criteria**, written as Given / When / Then;
- **test cases**: exact commands your trainer runs, with the expected result;
- **subtasks**, each owned by a squad role (Backend, Data, QA/DevOps, Scrum/Docs, or every student);
- a **definition of done** and a **points** value (1 to 13).

A ticket moves Backlog, To Do, In Progress, Review, Done, and **only a trainer can move it to Done**. When they do, they give it a gate score from 0 to 3. The tickets marked D01 to D11 are defences: you present the work and answer questions on it.

:::key Two things decide your capstone marks
First, the trainer runs the ticket's test cases, so run them yourself before you ask for review. Second, everything is judged from commit evidence: if the work is not in your own commits, pull requests and reviews, it does not count for you.
:::

Part 8 of this book has a guide for every ticket: what it asks, how each test case works, how to split the work so your contribution shows, how to do it step by step, and how to defend it. Read each guide the evening before its session.

## How the viva is scored

During the capstone you are questioned every day. Each viva gives 0 to 5 on four things, for a total out of 20:

1. **Acceptance criteria met**: does your work do what the task said?
2. **Technical depth**: can you explain how it works underneath?
3. **Contribution and ownership**: is it visibly your work, in your commits?
4. **Understanding**: can you answer "why" and "what if" questions about it?

Every day chapter ends with a section called "Say it out loud". Practise those answers aloud, not in your head. The final capstone grade is 60% your own contribution and 40% your team's result, so both your commits and your teammates matter.

## A fair way to use this book

The practice questions and practice problems here are original. They train the same skills as the platform's quizzes and graded labs, but they are not copies of them, and the book does not contain the platform's answers. Each Day chapter restates its graded lab in plain words and shows how it is marked, then solves a different "twin" problem with the same skills. The ticket guides teach you to do each ticket and show every technique, but they do not hand you finished files. What you submit is your own work, which is also the only kind of work that survives a viva.

```cards
First attempt :: The attempt that sets your skill score. Prepare before it, not after.
Hidden test :: A test you cannot see that checks edge cases such as empty input, ties and missing values.
Pass mark :: 70% on quizzes and assessments. Aim for 90% in practice so you have a margin.
Part G :: The five assessed challenges at the end of each milestone project, each with a "Done when" test.
Viva :: A spoken examination of your own work, scored 0 to 5 on four dimensions.
Commit evidence :: Your Git history under your own identity. Project work is scored from it.
```
